# A Scholarly Critique of *Essentials of Moral Realism*

A hostile-but-fair review of the chapters in `docs/Morality/`. Ordered by severity: the first five are load-bearing failures — if they aren't answered, the book's thesis doesn't stand.

---

## I. Fatal problems

### 1. The is–ought gap is never crossed; the central claim is a tautology

The book's whole argument reduces to: *good = conducive to tribal survival*. But it never argues for this identity — it stipulates it, most nakedly at `objective-morality.md:56` ("Goodness Is Equated With Existence").

Once you stipulate it, everything downstream is true by definition and nothing has been discovered. `introduction.md:16` states the thesis as **"we discovered the best way to live because it is the best way to live."** That is offered as a succinct summary; it is in fact a bare tautology that survives only by equivocating on "best" — best-for-survival in one occurrence, morally-best in the other. The entire book rides on that equivocation.

Worse: the book *raises* the killing objection and then doesn't answer it. `what-is-good.md:61-67` asks, "This might be shown to be good for you, and good for your tribe, but is it really _good_ in the moral sense?" — which is Moore's open question, the single most important objection to naturalistic moral realism. The response is to attribute the question to theists ("This argument is appealing to the mystery of morality... an action can only be moral if commanded by a superior being") and then pivot to Euthyphro. That is a genetic fallacy. The open question is asked just as forcefully by Hume, Moore, Mackie, and Street — none of whom are theists, none of whom are cited. A secular subjectivist can grant every empirical claim in this book (yes, murder-tolerant tribes fare badly) and deny the conclusion entirely.

**What the book needs:** an argument that survival/flourishing is where the "ought" bottoms out — Foot's and Hursthouse's *natural goodness*, or Railton, or Boyd's Cornell realism, would be the obvious allies. Instead the gap is papered over with an unmarked definition.

### 2. The flagship analogy contradicts the book's own paradigm case of subjectivity

`what-is-good.md:45-51` argues by analogy from sugar: sweetness is pleasurable not because sugar has energy but because those who found it pleasurable survived; the same, mutatis mutandis, for moral sense.

But sweetness is the textbook **response-dependent** property. Sugar is not *objectively delicious*; there is no sweetness in the molecule. The analogy therefore establishes that moral sense is an evolved *response*, not that it tracks an objective property — which is exactly Sharon Street's Darwinian Dilemma and the Ruse/Wilson debunking argument, deployed here in reverse without noticing.

And the internal contradiction is direct: `definitions.md:8` stipulates that "if something tastes good, we will call it delectable," and `subjective-morality.md:19-21` uses **flavor preference as the definitive example of a subjective claim** — "Cherry Garcia... nobody can say you are wrong." So taste is the book's paradigm of subjectivity in one chapter and its paradigm of objectivity-through-evolution in another. Both cannot stand.

The evolutionary story also explains our moral *beliefs* without requiring them to be *true* — which is what a debunker needs. The book must show the evolutionary process is *truth-tracking*, not merely fitness-tracking, and it never attempts this.

### 3. The boat and dog analogies establish only hypothetical, not categorical, normativity

`objective-truths.md:11-27` is the book's central positive argument: boat-design truths are objective and uncreated, therefore moral truths can be too.

But the chapter concedes the fatal qualification in its own text at `objective-truths.md:23`: *"different designs will be optimal for different purposes... The design is related to the purpose."* Boat-design facts are **hypothetical imperatives** — *if* you want speed, *then* a catamaran. They are objective facts about means. Morality is supposed to be **categorical**: binding regardless of what you want.

So the analogy proves only that instrumental truths are objective — which no subjectivist denies. The subjectivist's reply writes itself: "Agreed. And which purpose to adopt is precisely the subjective part." The book has argued for the uncontroversial half and left the contested half untouched. The dog section (`objective-truths.md:29-39`) repeats the error: "the optimal way for a dog to act" is optimal *relative to* pack survival.

The dog section also has factual problems. "A dog does not kill another dog in the pack" and "dogs respect property rights" (`objective-truths.md:31`) are both false as stated — lethal intra-pack aggression and infanticide are documented in wolves and free-ranging dogs. And calling behavioral regularities "moral truths" (`objective-truths.md:37`) assumes exactly what's in dispute (the de Waal / Kitcher debate).

### 4. The Nazi argument is a *reductio* of the book's own criterion

`objective-morality.md:50`: "Occasionally societies appear with bad morals, like the Nazis, but they died out because their morals were bad. So did the communists. The fact that they died out is evidence (maybe proof) that their morals were wrong."

This paragraph is the most damaging in the book, for five independent reasons:

- **It entails might-makes-right.** Nazi Germany was destroyed by external military force. If survival tracks moral truth, then had the Wehrmacht won, Nazi morality would have been *correct*. The theory has no resources to deny this, and a moral theory that makes the outcome of a war constitutive of moral truth has refuted itself.
- **It contradicts the book six lines earlier.** `objective-morality.md:44` says "it may take thousands of generations to actually determine whether a particular action is good or bad." The Nazi regime lasted twelve years. You cannot claim millennial timescales and then read a verdict off a decade — and off a sample of n=1 with no control.
- **The premise is factually shaky.** "So did the communists" — the PRC, Vietnam, Laos, and Cuba persist; the USSR lasted ~70 years. Meanwhile chattel-slave societies persisted for millennia and empires built on conquest lasted centuries. By this criterion, slavery was moral for most of recorded history and only became immoral around 1865 — a conclusion the book explicitly rejects at `definitions.md:84` and `christian-morality.md:32`.
- **"Evidence (maybe proof)"** — no. Survivorship correlation across one uncontrolled case is not proof of anything.
- **It contradicts the book's escape hatch.** `objective-morality.md:44` says "we cannot isolate one moral rule from the others so it might be impossible to tell which rule caused a particular tribe to fail." That holism is invoked to protect the theory from falsification, then abandoned whenever a verdict is wanted.

### 5. The theory is unfalsifiable, and the book knows it

`objective-morality.md:44` and `:50`: individual rules cannot be tested, "so we have to treat the morals all together." Combined with `objective-morality.md:40-46` ("Existing Is Not Knowing") and multi-millennial timescales, the theory becomes untestable in principle.

Yet the book issues confident verdicts throughout: murder, theft, lying, bloodletting, slavery, hand-chopping, UA93, the Nazis. And at `call-to-action.md:12` it concedes the reverse — "It could be that everyone is better off... if we execute our worst offenders." If the epistemology is that weak, *none* of the verdicts are licensed, and the book has not delivered what it promised at `subjective-morality.md:49`: "the higher moral ground from which to persuade the right action." It delivers a metaphysics whose practical payoff is deferred indefinitely — the subjectivist and the objectivist are left in exactly the same position when arguing about capital punishment tomorrow.

`objective-morality.md:66` compounds this: "I don't know of any examples of moral rules that are detrimental to the success of the tribe." This is a universal empirical claim asserted with zero evidence, and it's false. Obvious counterexamples: mandatory celibacy (the Shakers went *extinct* obeying a moral rule), ritual human sacrifice, honor killing, widow-burning, foot-binding, caste endogamy, blood feud. Each is a moral rule with a plausible net fitness cost. An unengaged counterexample class this large is a scholarly liability, not a footnote.

## II. Serious problems

### 6. Equivocation on "objective" — and the theory is covertly relativist

`definitions.md:54-60` defines objective as "outside an individual mind... observable by any number of people the same way." That is *intersubjective descriptive accessibility*. Moral realism requires far more: mind-independent **normative** facts. Facts about what promotes group survival satisfy the book's definition trivially and the realist's requirement not at all. The definition has been chosen so as to make the conclusion cheap.

More seriously, the theory the book actually defends is *relativist* by its own lights:

- `definitions.md:70` — the Moral Optimum is objective, "however different actions might be optimum at different places and times."
- `rules.md:74` — "Morality for an Inuit may be different than morality for a Tahitian."
- `living-morally.md:20` — "morality is a property of the universe that is related directly to our physical form."

That is moral truth indexed to species, physiology, environment, and era. The rescue at `rules.md:74` — "a single complex rule that applies differently to different environment" — is unfalsifiable: any observed variation can be absorbed into the unstated complexity of a rule nobody can state. Meanwhile `moral-relativism.md` attacks relativism without ever distinguishing it from this position. A relativist would say the book has conceded the case and merely relabelled it.

### 7. Subjectivism and relativism are both straw-manned

`subjective-morality.md:23`: "If a moral claim was subjective, then whatever you claim would by definition be true."

This is *simple speaker subjectivism*, the crudest member of the family, and essentially nobody defends it. The book never engages: expressivism, Blackburn's quasi-realism (built specifically to preserve moral error and moral argument without realism), constructivism (Korsgaard, Scanlon), ideal-observer subjectivism, or Mackie's error theory. Most of these explicitly allow that you can be *wrong*. The book's core objection — "your claim about morality can be right or wrong, and that is what tells us that morality is not subjective" (`subjective-morality.md:49`) — is answered by nearly every serious anti-realist position on offer, none of which appear.

`moral-relativism.md:16` asserts without argument: "If you truly believe that morality is subjective, then you must accept the reality of moral relativism as well." This is a non-sequitur, and false on the book's own definitions — individual subjectivism and cultural relativism are distinct positions with different truth-makers. And `moral-relativism.md:18` ("how can bloodletting be both moral and immoral at the same time, for different people?") has a standard relativist answer via indexing (*moral-for-Bob* / *moral-for-Sam* are different predicates, no contradiction) that the chapter never states, let alone rebuts. At 260 words, this chapter is too thin to carry a load-bearing "trap."

### 8. The hand-chopper parable begs the question, and its rebuttal is self-defeating

`hand-choppers.md:21-31` shows that the law devastates the tribe, then concludes "It is an objectively immoral law." But the premise the subjectivist denies is not *that the law causes harm* — they'll grant that cheerfully — it's *that harm-to-survival constitutes immorality*. The parable establishes an empirical fact and simply relabels it a moral one. It cannot convince anyone not already convinced. It is question-begging in precisely the classic sense.

The response to the virus objection at `hand-choppers.md:37` is worse. Dismissing it as "a completely unrealistic hypothetical" misunderstands what thought experiments do — the objection probes whether the verdict is contingent on consequences. And the book's own consequentialism *requires* the answer "yes, in that context chopping would be good" — which is a **concession the author should welcome**, since it confirms the framework. Instead the objection is brushed aside in a way that flatly contradicts the book's own central "context matters" thesis (`rules.md:46-60`, `what-is-good.md:21-33`). This is the single most self-defeating passage in the book.

### 9. "Moral Propositions don't Matter" is self-refuting and misstates the literature

`what-is-good.md:69-77` argues that no moral proposition can be true or false because propositions can never be complete.

- **Self-refuting:** the book's own central propositions — "objective moral truths exist," "the hand-chopping law is objectively bad" — are then neither true nor false. The thesis eats itself.
- **Proves too much:** the same incompleteness argument voids every empirical proposition. "Water boils at 100°C" omits pressure, purity, and gravity. We don't conclude chemistry has no truths.
- **Factual error about the field:** "moral nihilists (a.k.a. error theorists) claim that no moral statement can ever be made, because those statements are actually about imperative commands, wishes, or desires." That is **non-cognitivism/emotivism** (Ayer, Stevenson, Hare). Error theory (Mackie) holds the opposite: moral statements *are* truth-apt propositions, and are uniformly *false*. Conflating the two, and equating both with nihilism, will be spotted immediately by any reader who knows the literature, and it undermines confidence in the rest.

Note also that `living-morally.md:35-37` correctly describes Prescriptivism as the view that moral judgements are orders — the same position it mislabels as error theory two chapters earlier.

### 10. Act- and rule-consequentialism are conflated throughout

`what-is-good.md:12` asserts consequentialism flatly, without defense: "The moral value of that act is strictly about the consequence of that act." But the book then judges, interchangeably:

- individual acts (the sniper, `rules.md:56`; UA93, `rules.md:68`)
- laws (`hand-choppers.md`)
- entire moral systems (`objective-morality.md:50`)
- rules "on average across many people and in the appropriate situations" (`what-is-good.md:67`)

These are *different theories* with different verdicts — act vs. rule consequentialism is one of the oldest fault lines in ethics, and the book slides across it without noticing. `what-is-good.md:67`'s "on average" is rule-consequentialism and directly contradicts `what-is-good.md:12`'s "strictly about the consequence of that act."

The exclusion of mental states ("Thinking is not an action. Imagining is not an action," `what-is-good.md:10`) also imports the standard consequentialist embarrassments, none addressed: an attempted murder that fails harms no one and is therefore not bad; a bungler whose selfishness accidentally saves a village acted well. Any reviewer will ask.

### 11. The scope argument silently switches to intuition — the very foundation being rejected

`what-is-good.md:53-59` escalates the beneficiary from agent → tribe → all humanity → animals → "some kind of universal perspective." But the survival criterion is defined at the **tribe** level, and tribal survival routinely *conflicts* with humanity's and with animals' interests. The book notices the conflict ("throwing waste on another tribe's land") and then resolves it by appeal to *"There is a sense that..."* and *"There is a feeing that..."* — i.e., bare moral intuition.

That is the subjective foundation the book set out to eliminate, smuggled back in at the exact point where the evolutionary criterion gives the wrong answer. And the concluding sentence — "a good action is fundamentally good in itself" — is **non-naturalism**, an entirely different metaethics from the naturalism defended everywhere else.

### 12. Group selection is assumed, and the free-rider problem is unanswered

"Tribes survive or die based on their moral rules" (`objective-morality.md:52`) is group selection, which is contested in evolutionary biology (Williams, Dawkins vs. Sober/Wilson) and never acknowledged as contested. No mechanism is given for how good morals spread, and individual selection generally favors defection — the free-rider gets the benefits of others' morality without the costs. The book never mentions kin selection, reciprocal altruism, costly signalling, or cultural group selection, which are the actual candidate mechanisms.

`love.md` appears to be where this is answered, but it doesn't answer it — and it self-contradicts within four lines: `love.md:8` defines love as "that propensity for humans to do more for someone than they expect to receive in return" (altruism), and `love.md:12` says "Living a moral lifestyle is not altruistic." Love is named "the human superpower that makes morality work" (`love.md:8`) — naming a thing is not explaining it.

### 13. Key arguments are true by construction of their terms

`rules.md:32-34` gives an excellent caution: action-descriptions embed moral judgments ("embezzling," "jaywalking," "cheating"), so "one has to be careful to isolate the action to purely what was done, and not a description that selects according to a judgement" (`what-is-good.md:41`).

The book then violates this repeatedly. `objective-morality.md:30-32` defines murder as *"arbitrary killing of fellow tribe members"* and discovers that a tribe endorsing it collapses. Of course it does — "arbitrary" did all the work. No society has ever permitted arbitrary intra-group killing, so this is not a discovered moral truth but a definitional one. `christian-morality.md:59`'s "A society that says it is OK to lie will die out. It is as simply as that" has the same shape and is empirically false besides — no society bans all lying, and the book itself endorses lying to the Gestapo at `rules.md:58`.

## III. Weaknesses in the theism chapters

### 14. Euthyphro is handled inconsistently, and the book concedes the theist's escape

`what-is-good.md:65` presents the dilemma correctly. But `christian-morality.md:51` then claims the view "completely eliminates the Euthyphro Dilemma." It does no such thing — it *takes the first horn*. Eliminating a dilemma and choosing a horn are different things.

More damaging, `christian-morality.md:93-99` concedes that Turek's "God's nature" formulation is the **standard third-horn reply** and that "there's no functional difference between the nature of the universe and the nature of God." If the author grants that, then Euthyphro no longer refutes the sophisticated theist — yet it is deployed as a weapon two chapters earlier. Either the concession is wrong or the weapon is blunt; the book can't have both, and doesn't notice the tension.

### 15. Genetic fallacy — used against theism while being denied against morality

`christian-morality.md:26`: "It has been that pattern in all primitive cultures that when those people can not imagine the source of something... they then simply conclude that something must have made it."

This is a causal story about *why people believe* divine command theory, offered as a reason to think it *false*. That's a genetic fallacy. Note the exact symmetry with the book's own predicament: the author needs a causal-evolutionary story about why we hold moral beliefs to be *compatible* with those beliefs being true (§2 above), while treating a causal story about theistic belief as *sufficient* to discredit it. You cannot have that both ways in the same volume.

### 16. Rhetoric undercuts the scholarly register

`introduction.md:12`: Christians and Muslims "each arrogantly claim... readily spew invectives calling everyone else is baseless immoral heathens." `christian-morality.md:41` is titled "Righteous Arrogance." `objective-morality.md:64` calls religious practices "superstitions."

Whatever one thinks of the substance, this is self-defeating in a book whose thesis is that moral judgments should be evaluated impartially against consequences. A reader in the target audience — the "believing friends" addressed at `call-to-action.md:16` — will stop reading. The quotes from Craig, Lewis, and Henderson (`christian-morality.md:63-79`) receive one-line replies apiece, none of which engage the actual argument. Craig's claim in particular is about grounding, and "Even without God, we can appeal to something outside of our subjective judgments" is an assertion of the very thing under dispute.

## IV. Scholarship and craft

**No engagement with the moral realist literature the book claims to join.** Absent entirely: Moore's open question, Hume's is/ought (both directly load-bearing), Mackie (queerness, relativity), Street's Darwinian dilemma, Railton, Boyd/Brink/Sturgeon's Cornell realism, Foot and Hursthouse on natural goodness — the last being the tradition most likely to *rescue* this argument. Parfit and Singer appear only as YouTube links (`morality-references.md:26,34`). Sam Harris's *The Moral Landscape* makes nearly this argument and is not mentioned; the objections it drew are exactly the ones above.

**Contradictory definitions.** `definitions.md:18` defines **Morals** as "personal beliefs about right and wrong... They are more subjective" and **Ethics** as "more objective." That's a conventional dictionary gloss that directly fights the book's thesis, and it's contradicted by the four-layer model further down the same page (`definitions.md:70-76`), where morals sit at layer 3 and objectivity at layer 1.

**Incomplete chapters shipped as content.** `living-morally.md:39-40` — "Social Contract (John Rawls)" and "Stoic Cosmopolitanism" are bare headings with no text, despite both being promised in the chapter's own list. `morality-references.md:16-18` lists "John Rawls" and "Massimo Pigliucci" as references with no work cited.

**`christian-morality.md:81-101` ("Sundry") appears to be unedited dictation** and should not ship in this state: "the source of mortality" for *morality*; "God decided that ex is moral and why is immoral" (presumably *X* and *Y*); "Turk" for *Turek*; "or lease will be created"; "That will also admit"; "that may not strike you immediately yes being in intelligence." The evolution-as-intelligence excursus in the final two paragraphs is also off-topic and, if anything, hands ammunition to the intelligent-design position the book opposes.

**Structural issues.** `intro.md` and `Morality/index.md` are byte-identical duplicates apart from an image path and a video link. `hand-choppers.md` has no frontmatter, so it has no sidebar position. The `introduction.md:20-38` chapter list doesn't match the actual `sidebar_position` ordering (Rules is chapter 4 in the files but is not in the introduction's list at all; Love is 8th in the list and position 10 in the files). Recurring typos: "therefor," "Therefor," "we can't just the description" (`what-is-good.md:77`), "Other tribe how different combinations" (`objective-morality.md:44`, duplicated verbatim at `:50`), "intractible," "towared," "a feeing."

## V. What I'd fix first

If I were advising on a revision, in priority order:

1. **Confront the is/ought gap explicitly**, in its own chapter, and pick a strategy — most plausibly neo-Aristotelian natural goodness (Foot), which grounds "good" in the flourishing of a kind of organism and is the closest existing position to what's being argued here. Right now the book's central move is invisible to the author.
2. **Cut the Nazi/communist passage** or replace it with a defensible version. As written it is a gift to any critic: it entails that winning a war makes you right.
3. **Answer the "so it's relativism" charge head-on.** The book indexes moral truth to species, physiology, era, and environment, then attacks relativism. That needs an explicit account of the difference.
4. **Fix the sugar analogy** — as written it is an argument *against* the thesis, and it contradicts the ice-cream example in the very next chapter.
5. **Steel-man subjectivism.** Engage quasi-realism and constructivism, both of which grant moral error. The current target — "whatever you claim is true by definition" — is a position nobody holds.
6. **Rewrite the virus reply in `hand-choppers.md`** to concede the hypothetical, which supports rather than threatens the framework.
7. **Copy-edit `christian-morality.md`**, finish `living-morally.md`, and de-escalate the rhetoric in `introduction.md:12` — the intended audience includes the people being called arrogant.

The underlying intuition — that moral facts can be objective, mind-independent, and wholly natural — is a respectable and actively-defended position. The book's problem is not that the thesis is wrong; it's that the hardest objections to it are either unaddressed, misattributed to theists, or answered by restating the thesis.
